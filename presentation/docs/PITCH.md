# Pitch deck outline — FEM ultrasound simulation vs k-Wave

13 slides, ~15 min. Figures are under `data/results/`. Companion documents:
`presentation/docs/brief.html` (decision brief) and `dossier.html` (technical detail).

Everything below is measured, on disk, and regenerated by a script. No hand-drawn figures.

---

## 1. The inspection problem

**Say:** A robot inside an oil pipe has to find cracks on the wall's *far* side and measure how
deep they are. 9.5 mm steel wall, 20 mm of water standoff, a 256-element ultrasound array. The
target here is a 4.0 mm deep crack on the outer wall - 42% through-wall.

**The point:** the answer we need is a **time**, not a brightness. Depth is inferred from when
echoes arrive. That drives every technical choice.

*Figure: schematic, or text only.*

## 2. Why the beam is steered 20 degrees

**Say:** Fire straight at a 1 mm crack and it is almost invisible. So we tilt to 20 degrees. At
that angle Snell's law kills the compression wave in the steel entirely - past its 15.3 degree
critical angle - and leaves an almost pure **shear** wave at 45 degrees. That shear beam skips
off the far wall and strikes the crack side-on, where it scatters strongly.

**The point:** the physics is deliberate, not incidental. This is the mode the research team's
imaging method (`TT-T`) is built on.

*Figure: `viz/wavefield_snap_p20deg_t27p0us.png`*

## 3. The wavefield - play this

**Say:** Compression in the water, shear in the steel, mode conversion at the inner wall, the
half-skip off the outer wall, and scattering at the crack tip.

**Caveat to state:** this animation is degree 3 and illustrative; the published numbers use
degree 4 on a finer mesh.

*Figure: `viz/wavefield_snap_p20deg.gif` (12.7 MB) or `wavefield_web.gif` (5.4 MB)*

## 4. Same physics, two ways to approximate space

**Say:** Both codes solve one equation - Newton's law plus Hooke's law for a solid. They differ
only in how they represent space. k-Wave lays a uniform 50 um grid and takes derivatives by FFT.
We use a mesh whose cell edges lie **on** the true curved walls and crack faces.

**Be fair here:** k-Wave's method is genuinely excellent in smooth uniform material - better than
FEM per unit of computation. We do not claim otherwise. The difference is geometry: a grid cannot
hold a hole, so their crack is filled with fictitious 500 m/s material where ours is a true void
with the physically correct traction-free surface.

*Figure: `viz/mesh_zoom.png` - conforming arc at 0.03 um vs a 50 um staircase, and the notch void*

## 5. Making it a fair fight

**Say:** We run **their** beamformer on **our** data. Their imaging code is a Python package in
the research team's repository and we call it directly, unmodified, on both datasets - with the
same options their own simulation script passes, which we audited line by line and corrected.

**The point - this is the strongest design decision in the project:** because the imaging half is
identical for both, any difference in the final image is attributable to the forward solver alone.
It removes the obvious objection that we simply wrote a better image processor.

*Figure: none. A two-box diagram works: two solvers, one arrow into one beamformer.*

## 6. Does our physics actually work? Exact-solution tests

**Say:** Before comparing against anyone, we check against problems with known closed-form
answers. Oblique mode conversion against the exact fluid-solid system: **0.8%** error at the
production angle, shear angle correct to **0.55 degrees**. Scattering from a defect against the
exact Pao & Mow series: **~1%**, with **zero fitted parameters**.

**The point:** the research team's scripts contain no convergence, CFL or dispersion study at all.
Rigour here is itself a differentiator.

*Figures: `zoeppritz/amplitude.png` + `cavity_scattering/dscf_vs_exact.png`*

## 7. Head-to-head

**Say:** Both datasets, one beamformer, known ground truth - we chose the crack, so accuracy is
measurable rather than a matter of opinion.

| metric (truth) | k-Wave +20 | **FEM +20** | k-Wave -20 | **FEM -20** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| crack depth (4.0 mm) | 3.48 (-13%) | **3.73 (-6.8%)** | 3.85 (-3.8%) | **3.85 (-3.8%)** |
| crack position error | 0.41 mm | **0.16 mm** | 0.33 mm | **0.08 mm** |
| crack / clutter RMS | 24.0 dB | **26.5** | 24.5 dB | **26.3** |
| crack / worst clutter | 11.6 dB | **14.1** | 11.8 dB | **13.5** |

**FEM better in 25 of 30 robustness checks** across both angles. Lead with the part that is
unanimous: **contrast, 10/10** at both angles and every guard distance. Sizing is 15/20 - we win
+20 deg outright, and -20 deg is an exact tie at 3.85 mm. Say the tie out loud.

*Figure: `compare/compare_p20deg.png`*

## 8. The result to lead with - consistency across angle

| notch depth, true 4.0 mm | +20 deg | -20 deg | spread |
|---|---|---|---|
| k-Wave | 3.48 | 3.85 | **11%** |
| **FEM** | 3.73 | 3.85 | **3%** |

**Say:** Their sizing moves 11% when the beam angle flips; ours moves 3%. Both datasets go
through the same imaging chain, so an imaging quirk would move both equally.

**Say this too - it is the most credible thing on the slide.** An earlier version of this table
claimed 96% against 3%. We found the cause: our wrapper was not passing the migration
anti-aliasing that their own script passes. Fixing it repaired *their* -20 deg number from
6.33 mm to 3.85 mm and cut our own claimed advantage by a factor of eight. We corrected it before
presenting it. A team that audits its own favourite result is a team you can trust with the rest.

**The point:** a 3.7x advantage in angle-consistency is still the right thing to care about. A
real crack is never conveniently on-axis, and a sizing error that swings with beam angle is one
you cannot calibrate away.

## 9. Prediction, then test - why this is science

**Say:** Before the second angle was solved, we wrote down what it had to produce for our
explanation to hold: clutter 23-25 dB, extent 3.7-4.2 mm. Measured: **24.0 dB and 3.85 mm**. Both
inside the predicted range.

**The point:** that makes the mechanism out-of-sample tested rather than a story fitted to one
result. It is the difference between a demo and a finding.

## 10. The crack is real, not wall structure

**Say:** We ran the same simulation on a defect-free wall. In that image the crack region sits
**6.1 dB below** the worst clutter elsewhere - there is no feature there at all. The cracked run
puts **+16.24 dB** in the same region. Subtracting the two leaves the crack and darkens the wall.

**Honest limit:** removing the notch changes the mesh and therefore the time step, so the two runs
are not an exact null. Measured noise floor 2.3% of the difference peak - usable, with ~13 dB of
headroom, but not zero.

*Figure: `compare/baseline_subtract_20deg.png`*

## 11. "Your images have artifacts" - the objection, answered

**Say:** An artifact is brightness where nothing real exists. The standard is that a defect-free
steel wall must image black. **Neither image is black - and ours is the cleaner of the two**
(26.5 vs 24.0 dB crack-to-clutter, 14.1 vs 11.6 dB against the worst pixel), unanimously at both
angles and every guard distance.

**Why ours nonetheless looks dirtier:** we plot on a -40 dB **log** scale; their published images
are **linear** min-max. Our clutter sits at 14% of the crack peak - a faint grey on a linear
scale, mid-palette bright on log. Same data.

**Concede honestly:** we have eliminated four candidate causes by measurement - the absorbing
boundary (two independent fixes), mesh coarsening, sample-rate aliasing, and the mass matrix.
A fifth, migration-operator aliasing, removed most of the excess at +20 deg but not at -20 deg,
so it contributes without explaining. **We can say what it is not; we cannot yet say what it is**,
and it is worse at -20 deg (+3.5 dB) than at +20 deg (+0.5 dB).

**One artifact is definitely not ours, and we can derive it:** the array pitch is 0.30 mm and
their own script's no-grating-lobe limit at 20 degrees steering is 0.279 mm. A full-amplitude
alias of the main lobe therefore exists by array geometry alone, in both simulations. Their code
also notes it cannot be removed by apodization.

## 12. What we cannot claim

**Say this slide out loud. It is what makes the rest believable.**

- **Sizing: solid.** Converged and unanimous across analysis choices.
- **Contrast: the strongest claim, and a lower bound.** Unanimous 10/10 across both angles;
  still improving with refinement, so +2.4 dB is a floor, not a settled value.
- **Position: sub-pixel, not "2.5x better".** Our own value moved 0.24 mm between meshes and the
  image pixel is 0.248 mm. Quote it as "sub-pixel, <= 0.25 mm against their 0.41 mm".
- **Why we beat them is OPEN.** We tested both candidate explanations inside our own solver:
  staircasing the wall changed 0.61 dB, filling the crack 0.07 dB. Both eliminated. On raw
  bandwidth they are still **ahead** of us. "We measure better and cannot yet attribute it" is
  the honest position - and a stronger place to stand than a story that collapses under
  questioning.
- **One gate cannot be re-run.** The check that our imaging chain reproduces theirs passed once,
  against reference data since lost. What we can still show: our chain reproduces their published
  metrics to three significant figures.

## 13. Recommendation and next steps

**Say:** One scenario, two angles, fully validated, reproducible from committed code. The domain
expert who wrote the k-Wave simulation reviewed it and called it promising.

Costed next steps, cheapest first:

| step | cost | buys |
|---|---|---|
| Find the real cause of the edge clutter | days | the only place we lose; four causes now ruled out by measurement, and it is worse at -20 deg than +20 |
| A third angle, to settle sizing | ~2.4 h | sizing is 15/20 and tied at -20 deg; n=2 cannot separate a real edge from a coincidence |
| Ask research team for a defect-free k-Wave run | their time | the cleanest possible clutter-floor comparison |
| Third and fourth steering angles | ~2.4 h each | strengthens the angle-consistency claim |
| Convergence / GCI error bars | ~1 day compute | publishable numbers **with uncertainty**, which theirs do not have |
| Matrix-free solver | weeks | the real speed lever; measured bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not cores |

**Do not promise MPI speed-up.** It was estimated at 4-8x and measured at **1.71x** over what we
already run, because the operation is memory-bandwidth bound. Two estimates in this project were
wrong and were corrected by measurement; say that as a strength.

## Numbers you must not get wrong on stage

- Notch truth: **4.0 mm deep, 1.0 mm wide, at x = 38.25 mm**, on the outer wall, on-axis.
- Wall **9.5 mm**, standoff **20 mm**, **256** elements at 0.30 mm pitch, **4 MHz** 1-cycle burst.
- Steel **5700 / 3100 m/s**, water **1500 m/s**. Shear at **45 deg** from 20 deg incidence.
- Working configuration: **degree 4, scale 0.8**, 1.47 M unknowns, 163680 steps, **~2.4 h** per
  angle on 1 core.
- **Degree 3 LOSES** to k-Wave (12.2 dB). The configuration is not incidental.
- **0 degrees is not a valid third angle** - normal incidence produces almost no mode conversion,
  so neither solver images anything meaningful there.
